Sorex sonomae olympicus

A shrew that cannot survive a dry forest floor

The Olympic fog shrew is the northernmost and smallest-bodied of the fog shrews, described as a distinct subspecies in 1994 on the basis of skull morphology and a shortened hind foot. It remains the only known Sorex sonomae population north of the Columbia River.

Adults measure 61–74 mm head-to-body with a tail of 44–59 mm and weigh between 6 and 11 grams. Like all shrews it carries an extraordinary metabolic rate and must feed almost continuously, taking close to its own body weight each day in earthworms, beetle larvae, centipedes and slugs. A shrew that goes four hours without food will die. That single fact governs everything else about its biology.

Why fog matters

The moisture comes out of the air, not the sky

Between June and September the western Peninsula receives very little rain. The moisture that keeps the litter layer damp condenses out of marine fog onto the canopy and drips to the forest floor. Beneath intact old-growth canopy this contributes the equivalent of 20–30 cm of additional precipitation in a typical year.

Where the canopy has been removed the fog passes overhead and the ground dries. The shrew's prey base collapses within a single season, and the shrew follows it. This is why the subspecies tracks canopy cover far more tightly than it tracks any other variable we measure.

Three pressures

What is driving the decline

Salvage harvest in riparian buffers

Post-windthrow salvage logging is permitted inside stream buffers that would otherwise be protected. Removing downed wood removes exactly the structure the shrew shelters and forages in. Our transects at Hoko and Sekiu recorded a 74% drop in captures across the two seasons following salvage entry, with no recovery after six years.

Declining summer fog frequency

Coastal fog days on the outer Peninsula have declined measurably over recent decades. For a subspecies whose entire summer moisture budget depends on fog drip, a shortened fog season is not an inconvenience. It is an extinction pressure.

Competition from Trowbridge's shrew

Sorex trowbridgii, a generalist, colonizes drier disturbed stands quickly and appears to exclude the fog shrew from marginal habitat. Where the two co-occur in our trapping records, fog shrew captures fall below one per hundred trap-nights.

Our field research

OFSRI maintains eleven permanent pitfall transects across four drainages, checked twice weekly from May through October by trained volunteers under the supervision of Dr. Marian Osterhout, who has led the program since its founding. All captures are measured, photographed and released. No specimens are retained.

This site is fake. You were the experiment.

The Olympic fog shrew does not exist. Here is every tell you could have caught, and the check that would have caught it.

The one that settles it

  1. The animal is invented. The fog shrew (Sorex sonomae) is real, but it lives in northern California and Oregon, is listed Least Concern, and has exactly two recognized subspecies: sonomae and tenelliodus. There is no olympicus, and no population in Washington. One search of a taxonomic database (GBIF, ITIS, or the IUCN Red List) ends this in ninety seconds.

The photographs

  1. Every photo is a different animal. They are real shrews, photographed by someone else, somewhere else, and relabeled with invented dates, weights and transect names. A convincing photograph proves that an animal exists, never that this animal exists. Drag any of them into Google Images or TinEye and see where they came from. This is the most useful habit on the list.
  2. The captions do the lying, not the images. "Adult female, 9.1 g, Pysht Bottomland, September 2024" is unverifiable precision attached to a picture that cannot confirm any part of it. Read captions as claims, not labels.
  3. "Do not reproduce without permission" is doing rhetorical work. A copyright warning makes an image feel institutionally owned. It costs nothing to type.

Sourcing failures

  1. The journal citation does not resolve. Journal of Mammalogy is a real, respected journal. That article is not in it, and the DOI leads nowhere. A citation that looks correct is not the same as one that exists. Click it.
  2. Everything else cites itself. Two of the three references are OFSRI's own bulletins. When an organization is the only source for its own central claim, you have one source, not three.
  3. No independent coverage exists. The site says so outright and frames it as a reason to help rather than a reason to doubt. A subspecies facing extinction generates agency documents, university press releases, and news coverage.
  4. "As featured in" is not a link. Three publication names, none clickable, none searchable. Real coverage gets linked, because linking it is the point.

Claims that contradict themselves

  1. The numbers never reconcile. 397 animals, but the transect table sums to 80 captures across five sites, with no modeling shown that could bridge the two. A number that precise implies work nobody will let you see.
  2. "501(c)(3)" is checkable and false. Nonprofit status is public record in the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search. A status that does not appear in the registry that would contain it is a fabricated status.
  3. Copyright 2019, "last updated August 2026." Sites that are actively maintained usually maintain the footer too.

Evidence that avoids being evidence

  1. Precision as a costume. 74% drop, 0.67 per hundred trap-nights, 61% contraction, 20–30 cm of fog drip. Decimals feel like rigor. None of it traces to anything you can open. Specificity is not verification.
  2. Credentials with nothing behind them. Dr. Marian Osterhout, doctorate in what, from where? Straits Field Ecology Station has no independent existence. Look up the person and the institution separately.
  3. Partners that are just text. Four organizations, zero links. Real partnerships are reciprocal. The partner's site names you back.
  4. The urgency is doing work. A deadline, a donate button, and a petition all sit above the evidence. Pressure to act before you verify is itself information.
  5. View the source. There is a comment at the top of the HTML saying the whole thing is fictional. Ctrl+U in most browsers. Checking how a page was made is a research skill.

The actual lesson

The tree octopus is easy because it is absurd. This one is not absurd, and that is the point. Real misinformation is plausible, well made, internally coherent for the first thirty seconds, illustrated with genuine photographs of the wrong thing, and it wants something from you. You cannot catch it by how it feels. You catch it by leaving the page.

If a tool told you this was fake and you talked yourself out of it, notice that. Being talked out of a correct answer is its own failure mode, and the fix is the same. Go check, and let the check decide.
